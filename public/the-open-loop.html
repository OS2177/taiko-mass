The Open Loop

The body as process

There is a tendency to imagine the human body as a stable object — a form that persists, more or less intact, across time. But this is not how it operates.

The body is a process. It is cyclical, adaptive, and continuously resolving itself.

Breath moves in and out. The heart contracts and releases. Sleep descends and lifts. Cells form and decay.

At every scale, life unfolds through cycles — not as perfect circles, but as recursive patterns, returning again and again to complete what has begun.

To exist within such a system is not simply to move forward, but to continually return.

Every event initiates a response.

A sound is heard. A movement begins. An emotion rises. The nervous system mobilises.

In a stable environment, this response completes itself. Activation gives way to release. The body returns to baseline. The loop closes.

But not all processes are allowed to complete.

Interruption is common. Overwhelm, suppression, fear, or the absence of support can halt a response before it has finished.

The movement stops midway. The emotion is contained. The signal is overridden.

The system does not return. The loop remains open.

What remains open does not disappear. It persists.

Not as a narrative alone, but as a condition of the system itself — as tension in the body, as bias in perception, as pattern in behaviour.

The past is not held only in memory. It is held in state.

What is often called trauma can be understood in this way: not as the event, but as the persistence of an unfinished process.

Something began, and did not complete. The system continues to organise itself around this absence of closure.

There is a persistent belief that the system seeks completion above all else. This is not the case.

The system seeks survival first.

If completion threatens stability, the process remains open.

Non-closure is not failure. It is protection.

What is held open is held for a reason.

The system does not forget to close the loop. It decides, beneath awareness, that closing it is not yet safe.

Within this landscape, a stabilising structure takes form.

What we provisionally call the ego — though our understanding of it remains incomplete — appears to develop in relation to these open loops, serving the survival imperative that keeps them open.

It is not an error. It is an organiser.

Identity emerges, in part, from this structure. "I am this kind of person" may reflect not only preference or character, but the shape of what the system has learned to protect.

This organising self is not the source of fragmentation. It is what allows fragmentation to hold together.

Certain cultures understood something that has largely been lost.

That some loops cannot be closed alone.

That the transition from one state of being to another — from child to adult, from uninitiated to initiated — requires a structure larger than the individual to complete it.

Coming-of-age ritual functioned as exactly this.

Not as ceremony for its own sake, not as social bonding or cultural tradition alone, but as deliberate technology for loop closure.

The child-self, with its open developmental processes — dependency, boundlessness, the unresolved question of who one is in relation to the group — was brought, formally and often physically, to completion.

The old process finished. A new one began with a clear initiation point.

The community held what the individual could not hold alone. Completion was witnessed. The loop closed.

Cultures that have lost this are not simply losing tradition. They are losing the mechanism by which certain loops close at the right developmental moment.

And loops that do not close at the right moment do not disappear. They persist — as structure, as organising principle, as the self built around what did not resolve.

This may be why so much adult psychological work carries a ritualistic quality. Not from nostalgia, but because the system is still seeking the closure it did not receive.

The form keeps returning because the need never left.

What is sometimes called ego rigidity, or identity defensiveness, may be less a pathology and more a structural consequence of incomplete initiation.

These open processes do not remain isolated. They distribute.

Across the nervous system, across posture and movement, across attention and interpretation.

The organising self structures what it can. The body carries what the organising self structures.

The result is not a single point of disruption, but a field of partial activation — a life structured, often with great coherence, around what has not yet resolved.

Only when conditions of sufficient safety arise does the system begin to release what it has been holding.

Completion cannot be imposed. It can only be permitted.

Closure, when it occurs, is rarely conceptual. It is not achieved through explanation alone.

It happens when the interrupted process is allowed to finish — physically, emotionally, or perceptually.

A movement completes. A response discharges. A signal is acknowledged.

The system resolves what was left unresolved.

When a loop closes, something simple and measurable occurs: tension reduces, attention frees, capacity returns.

The past is not erased. It is completed.

A system with fewer open loops is not perfected. It is available.

Less occupied by unfinished processes, it regains access to presence, to perception, to creation.

This is not an ideal state. It is a functional one.

To live well is not to eliminate all interruption, but to develop the capacity to return — to close what can be closed, and to recognise what must remain open, for now.

The question is not whether one has open loops. Everyone does.

The question is whether one is organised around them, or able, gradually, to resolve them.

This is not a matter of force, nor of discipline in the conventional sense. It is a matter of completion.

A system that learns, even in small ways, to finish what it begins, begins to change.

Not dramatically, but structurally.

And from that shift, other forms of order become possible.
