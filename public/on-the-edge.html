On the Edge

Marginality as a driver of change

Introduction

This essay began with a simple observation.

Systems are often most visible to those who do not stand entirely inside them.

A person who belongs completely to a structure may experience its assumptions as reality. A person who stands entirely outside it may never understand how it actually feels to live within it.

But someone positioned at the boundary can experience both.

They participate in the system while remaining distant enough to observe its construction.

I began thinking about this position because it is one I recognize in myself. I have often felt close enough to society to understand its structures, but never entirely absorbed by the narratives that hold them together. From that position, patterns become visible that are more difficult to perceive from the centre.

As I began looking through Western history for other examples of this condition, I repeatedly encountered Jewish communities.

This was not because I began with a theory about Jewish people and attempted to build an argument around it. It was because their historical position within Western civilization so clearly reflects the condition I was trying to understand.

For centuries, Jewish communities lived inside European societies without being fully accepted as part of them. They were deeply involved in the economic, cultural and intellectual life of the West while remaining religiously, legally and socially distinct.

They belonged and did not belong.

They were inside and outside at once.

That prolonged position is historically exceptional, and it seems reasonable to ask what kinds of perception, intelligence and adaptation might develop from it.

It is also reasonable to ask what happens when capacities formed at the edge later move towards the centre. When marginality becomes prominence. When prominence becomes influence. When influence becomes organized power. And when the visible existence of that power becomes confused with the idea of complete collective control.

These are difficult questions because the history surrounding them is so heavily charged.

Jewish influence has often been exaggerated into mythology, used to assign collective intention to an entire people and weaponized through persecution. But the existence of that history cannot mean that every observation of Jewish prominence or influence must be avoided.

To refuse to examine a visible historical pattern out of fear that it may be misused is not clarity. Neither is turning that pattern into a total explanation.

The purpose of this essay is not to defend Jewish prominence, condemn it or reduce it to a single cause. It is to examine the structural position from which it emerged and to place it within a wider inquiry into how systems form, how narratives sustain them, and how those positioned at the edge — lateral or vertical — come to see what those at the centre cannot.

The Jewish case is central because it is central to Western history. But the edge belongs to no one people. It appears wherever a person or community lives within a system while remaining sufficiently separate from it to see that the system is not reality itself.

It is from that position that this essay begins.

I. Position

There is a place from which systems become visible.

It is not entirely inside them, where their assumptions are experienced as reality. Nor is it entirely outside them, where they can be dismissed without ever being understood.

It is at the boundary.

At the edge, participation and distance exist at once. You are close enough to feel the structure, but far enough away to see it.

From here, something subtle changes.

Events are no longer taken entirely at face value. Narratives are no longer simply believed or rejected.

They are observed.

II. Narrative and Structure

Human reality is not experienced through raw perception alone. It is continuously shaped by narrative.

Narrative names events, orders them and gives them meaning. It connects the present to the past and projects it into the future.

Most of the time, this process remains invisible. We do not experience narrative as something constructed around reality. We experience it as reality itself.

But from the edge, the construction becomes perceptible.

What appears to be stable truth is often the result of repetition, reinforcement and shared agreement. Different groups can inhabit radically different realities, not because the material world itself has changed, but because the structures through which it is interpreted have.

To perceive this does not mean that nothing is true.

It means that truth reaches us through frames, and that those frames have histories, interests and consequences.

III. The Edge as a Condition

The edge is not only a philosophical position. It has appeared repeatedly as a historical condition.

Certain individuals, communities and cultures have found themselves living inside dominant systems without ever being fully absorbed by them.

They participate in the structures around them, but remain distinct from the identity through which those structures understand themselves.

This position produces a particular kind of awareness.

When you are not fully embedded, you cannot rely entirely on inherited assumptions. You are required to interpret, translate and navigate between frameworks that do not completely align.

The outsider often learns the host system more deliberately than those born inside it. What others absorb unconsciously must be studied. What others experience as natural must be translated.

Over time, this can produce a heightened sensitivity to language, meaning and structure itself.

It does not guarantee wisdom. The edge can also produce alienation, suspicion and distortion.

But it creates the possibility of seeing what those more securely embedded are permitted to ignore.

IV. A Historical Pattern

One of the clearest and most sustained examples of this condition is found in the history of Jewish communities in Europe.

For centuries, Jewish people lived within Western societies while remaining separate from them in essential ways.

They participated in economic, intellectual and cultural life, but were repeatedly denied full social and political integration. They paid taxes to states that refused them equal citizenship. They served economies that often restricted their ownership of land or entry into established professions. They lived within legal systems that treated them as exceptions to the society around them.

This was not a passing or marginal experience. It was a central condition of Jewish life across generations: internal and external at once, involved yet distinct, necessary to the societies around them while never entirely belonging to them.

Jewish traditions of textual interpretation, legal reasoning and debate were far older than the European conditions in which they later existed. But exile, legal pluralism and the repeated need to navigate between internal communal life and external authority gave those traditions a particular historical force.

The Talmudic tradition is instructive here.

It does not simply record conclusions. It preserves arguments, disagreements and contradictory readings beside one another. Meaning is approached through interpretation, counter-interpretation and the sustained tension between positions that cannot be easily resolved.

This is more than an abstract intellectual preference.

When a community must continually navigate between its own law and the law of those who govern it, close reading becomes a form of survival. The difference between the stated rule and its actual application matters. The gap between the letter and the spirit becomes a place in which life must be negotiated.

Over centuries, this position helped cultivate a particular cognitive inheritance: attentiveness to language, suspicion of simple categories, comfort with contradiction and an instinct for reading beneath the surface of what is being said.

It would be absurd to reduce Jewish intellectual life to exclusion alone. Jewish history is too old, varied and internally complex for any single explanation.

But it would be equally absurd to pretend that centuries of living at the edge of Western society produced no lasting capacities.

V. Prominence

When Europe entered modernity, these capacities encountered a changing world.

Legal emancipation, urbanization, industrialization and the opening of universities and professions allowed Jewish people to enter fields from which they had previously been restricted.

What followed is historically striking.

Jewish thinkers, writers, scientists, lawyers, economists, artists, financiers and political figures became unusually prominent across many of the institutions through which modern Western society came to understand itself.

This prominence is not an invention.

Nor is it explained by one cause.

Literacy, education, urban concentration, family and communal networks, migration, exclusion from land ownership, experience across languages and cultures, and the intellectual habits formed through long traditions of interpretation all played some part.

The edge had produced capacities that modernity suddenly valued.

A people long required to read systems from a position of uncertainty entered an age increasingly governed by abstraction: law, finance, bureaucracy, psychology, media, markets and political ideology.

The tools developed at the edge became unusually powerful near the centre.

Prominence, however, does not remain merely visible.

It can become influence.

And influence, when sustained within particular institutions, can become a form of dominance within those fields.

This does not require an entire people to act as one. It does not require a hidden command or a single collective intention. Power rarely works so neatly.

It can emerge through networks, institutions, families, shared interests, historical relationships and the ordinary human tendency to work with those whose language and experience are familiar.

An influence can be real without being total.

A pattern can exist without every individual participating in it.

And a people can become highly prominent within a civilization without becoming a single political or cultural actor.

These distinctions matter, but they should not be used to deny the observation itself.

VI. Other Voices from the Threshold

The edge is not a condition unique to Jewish history.

It appears wherever individuals or groups must navigate between worlds without fully belonging to either.

The great translators of history developed analogous capacities. To translate is not merely to replace one word with another. It is to inhabit two structures of meaning at once and find a passage between them.

Migrants, exiles, colonized peoples and cultural intermediaries have repeatedly occupied this position.

Writers and artists often do too.

Kafka, writing in German as a Czech Jew in Prague, produced work that seems to exist inside and outside the system at the same time. His characters inhabit institutions whose rules appear both absolute and absurd. He understood the machinery from within, yet never belonged securely enough to mistake it for nature.

James Baldwin described American society with a precision sharpened by distance. He saw what it meant to be defined by a narrative you had not authored and could not simply escape.

W.E.B. Du Bois described double consciousness: the experience of seeing oneself through one's own perception while also being forced to see oneself through the eyes of the dominant society.

Simone Weil, Frantz Fanon and many others produced their clearest work because they could not take the prevailing frame for granted.

What these figures share is not one political identity.

It is a structural position.

They were required to see the machinery that others were allowed to ignore.

The edge is rarely comfortable.

But it can be generative.

VII. Contact with the Centre

Western civilization developed through the convergence of traditions.

Greek philosophy provided forms of logic and abstraction. Roman systems shaped law and governance. Christianity transformed the moral and symbolic life of Europe.

Within this, the Hebrew tradition became deeply embedded in Western consciousness.

Its texts entered Greek through the Septuagint. Its narratives and ethics passed into Christianity. Its understanding of law, covenant, exile, redemption and historical purpose became part of the architecture through which the West came to interpret itself.

This was not a simple act of one culture dominating another.

It was transmission through contact: the edge touching the centre and leaving behind traces that eventually became foundational.

A civilization increasingly organized through narrative came into prolonged contact with a people highly practised in navigating narrative.

Later, as Jewish people entered modern intellectual and institutional life, this interaction took on new forms.

Freud did not invent the unconscious from nothing. He brought to the study of mind a sensibility trained to read what is concealed beneath what is spoken, to treat the visible as a surface beneath which another structure may be operating.

Marx did not invent structural critique from nothing. He looked at the economic order from a position that did not accept its self-description and exposed the relations hidden beneath its language of freedom and exchange.

Kafka did not simply describe bureaucracy. He revealed the metaphysical atmosphere produced when systems become too large to be understood by those living inside them.

These thinkers cannot be reduced to their Jewishness. Nor should Jewishness be treated as incidental to the historical position from which they emerged.

Their work appeared at the meeting point between inheritance, marginality, individual genius and the structures of modern Western life.

VIII. Structure and Intention

Not all influence begins with intention.

Some of it emerges slowly through history, position and adaptation.

A way of thinking forms. A capacity develops. A community becomes unusually prepared for a world that did not exist when those capacities first appeared.

But once an advantage becomes visible, it does not remain unconscious forever.

What emerges without design can later be recognized, organized and used deliberately.

Prominence can become influence.

Influence can become institutional power.

Institutional power can be used to preserve itself, protect shared interests and shape the direction of the system around it.

This is where structure and agency begin to interact.

One forms the field.

The other moves within it.

When everything is treated as though it were designed from the beginning, the deeper historical process disappears. Analysis collapses into conspiracy because emergence is mistaken for planning.

But when all deliberate agency is denied, power disappears in the opposite direction. Everything becomes sociology, and nobody is ever responsible for anything.

Clarity requires both.

There are structures that no individual created.

There are also people and institutions that learn how to use them.

There are communities whose prominence arose through history, and organizations within those communities that later acted consciously in defence of particular interests.

The existence of emergence does not cancel intention.

The existence of intention does not prove total coordination.

A group does not need to behave as one body for parts of it to organize effectively.

This is especially visible where identity, history, security and political power meet. In the American relationship with Israel, for example, Jewish organizations, donors, advocates and political figures have exercised real agency in shaping policy and protecting the relationship between the two states.

That influence exists alongside Christian Zionism, military and strategic interests, defence industries, historical alliances and the wider machinery of American politics.

To acknowledge Jewish agency within that field is not to claim that Jewish people everywhere act with one mind.

It is simply to recognize that history produces interests, interests produce organization, and organization produces influence.

IX. The Misreading

The edge is often misunderstood.

From within the centre, it can appear unstable, ambiguous or threatening.

It does not affirm the dominant narrative, but neither does it always stand in simple opposition to it.

It reveals how the narrative was made.

This can feel like a loss of certainty.

And where certainty is destabilized, suspicion often follows.

But suspicion does not always arise from nothing.

Prominence can become visible. Influence can become real. Particular institutions can acquire disproportionate power. Networks can protect themselves and advance their interests.

The mistake begins when the power of particular people and institutions is converted into the collective agency of an entire people.

A Jewish organization is not all Jewish people.

A Jewish financier is not Jewish finance.

A Jewish media owner is not Jewish control of media.

But the existence of false generalization does not make every underlying observation false.

This is where the subject becomes difficult.

Real influence can produce an exaggerated perception of influence. A visible network can be imagined as an invisible totality. Institutional prominence can be transformed into a theory of complete ethnic coordination.

The perception may begin with something observable and end in something that no longer resembles reality.

The edge figure then becomes an object of suspicion, not only because they can see the machinery, but because some edge figures have acquired genuine power within it.

To those who remain fully immersed, the person who understands the frame can appear to be the person who built it.

Sometimes they did not.

Sometimes they helped reshape it.

Sometimes they now benefit from it.

These possibilities must be separated rather than collapsed into either innocence or accusation.

X. The Marginal Mind

Sociology has described this condition as that of the in-between figure: a person who belongs to more than one world but is not fully contained by either.

Robert Park called this figure the marginal man: someone living at the intersection of two cultures, unable to belong completely to either and therefore burdened with a kind of double vision.

From this position comes a particular capacity.

Each world reveals the assumptions of the other.

What one society treats as natural becomes visible as custom when placed beside another. What appears universal from inside one framework becomes local and historical when viewed from the boundary.

This does not grant certainty.

It often removes it.

But in doing so, it opens the possibility of clearer perception.

Not a view from nowhere.

A view from the threshold.

Perhaps that is as close to clarity as any situated being can reach.

XI. The Second Edge

Until now, the edge has been considered laterally: the cultural position of those who navigate between systems without fully belonging to any of them.

But there is another edge.

It runs vertically.

The very wealthy can exist at the boundary of society in a structurally different but related way.

They participate in its markets, laws, political structures and cultural life while remaining insulated from many of the consequences those systems impose on everyone else.

They do not experience the labour market as precarity in the ordinary sense. They do not encounter the legal system with the same degree of vulnerability. Economic collapse may alter their holdings, but it rarely threatens the immediate conditions of their existence.

This insulation is not merely comfort.

It is a structural position.

And like other structural positions, it changes perception.

When you are no longer subject to the ordinary pressures of a system, you can observe that system differently. You can take a longer view. You can move through its institutions without experiencing their narratives as absolute.

Ideas such as merit, opportunity, fairness and competition appear different when your survival no longer depends on believing in them.

The vertical edge can therefore produce its own kind of vision.

It reveals that many of society's narratives are functional. They create consent, organize behaviour and maintain stability.

They do not merely describe reality.

They help produce it.

To see this is to be in a position to use it.

XII. Two Edges, One Field

The lateral and vertical edges are not the same.

One arises from displacement, exclusion and the need to move between frameworks.

The other arises from insulation from the consequences that give those frameworks their force.

But both can produce distance from the system's self-understanding.

Both can create a relationship to narrative that is less immersive and more instrumental.

And both can make possible forms of movement unavailable to those who remain fully embedded.

Here, something important can occur.

The lateral edge figure — the interpreter, lawyer, analyst, financier, strategist, artist or intellectual — is often more legible to the vertical edge than to the centre.

The centre tends to experience narrative as reality.

Those at the edges are more likely to recognize it as construction.

This recognition can produce collaboration. It can produce patronage. It can produce entry into institutions of power for those whose analytical capacities are useful to the people who already command wealth and influence.

The vertical edge funds, employs or amplifies the capacities developed at the lateral edge.

The lateral edge, in turn, gains access to structures from which it was historically excluded.

No secret agreement is required.

It is enough that two positions, both capable of seeing beyond the surface narrative, find one another useful.

Over time, these relationships can become institutions.

Institutions create continuity.

Continuity becomes power.

XIII. The Commodity Condition

For those who remain fully embedded, another condition emerges.

They experience the system's narratives as reality. They inherit its categories without seeing them as categories. They organize their lives around ideas whose origins and purposes are rarely examined.

Desire itself is shaped within narrative.

What counts as success, failure, security, danger, beauty, respectability or freedom is not produced by raw perception alone. It is formed through the systems into which a person is born.

A person who experiences these framings as natural will often organize their life around them in predictable ways.

Predictable behaviour is manageable behaviour.

Manageable behaviour can be directed, measured and used.

This is the commodity condition.

It is not simply the reduction of a person to a thing. It is the reduction of agency to a range of responses that the system can anticipate and shape.

The person still chooses.

But the field within which the choice appears meaningful has already been constructed.

This does not feel like subjugation.

It feels like living.

XIV. The Collapsed Edge

There is a contemporary figure worth examining here: the social influencer.

On the surface, the influencer appears to occupy an edge position.

They stand apart from ordinary life. They comment on culture. They shape narrative. They appear to possess perspective.

But something structurally different has happened.

The influencer is not at the edge of the system.

They are at its most exposed interior.

Their existence is governed by engagement metrics, platform algorithms, brand partnerships and audience retention. Every perception they express passes through the question of how it will be received, measured, monetized and fed back into the next piece of content.

The system does not merely observe the influencer.

It runs through them.

What appears as voice can become signal optimization.

What appears as perspective can become audience mirroring.

What appears as standing apart can become the performance of standing apart, created for the consumption of those who remain entirely inside.

It is the aesthetic of the margin without the conditions that produce it.

The appearance of distance without genuine separation.

This is not necessarily a moral failure.

It is structural.

The edge requires friction with the system: exclusion, cost, uncertainty or a genuine gap between the observer and the observed.

When standing apart becomes profitable, the edge can collapse into performance.

And with it, the capacity it appeared to represent.

XV. The Function of the Edge

The edge is not a place to remain in isolation.

Its function is not withdrawal.

It is translation.

To observe how systems form, how narratives sustain them, how prominence becomes influence and how influence interacts with intention.

And then to return that insight in a form that can be received.

Without this movement, the edge becomes distance without consequence.

With it, the edge becomes a point of contact through which systems may begin to recognize themselves.

Those inside them may begin to see the frame around the picture they have been living in.

And once the frame becomes visible, it becomes possible to ask whether it still deserves to remain.

XVI. Return

The edge is not owned by any one group.

It appears wherever conditions create distance within participation: in migrants and exiles who carry two worlds at once; in artists who are permitted, and sometimes required, to say what cannot otherwise be said; in thinkers working at the boundary between disciplines, where the assumptions of one system become visible against another; and in those who have fallen through the categories a society uses to recognize itself.

But within Western history, the Jewish experience remains exceptional.

Few communities have existed for so long within the centre of a civilization while remaining so persistently distinct from it.

Few have been so deeply woven into its religious imagination while remaining socially separate.

Few have moved so repeatedly between exclusion and prominence, vulnerability and influence, persecution and institutional success.

That history deserves to be examined without fear and without mythology.

It should not be flattened into innocence.

Nor should it be distorted into collective guilt.

The point is not to decide whether the edge is good or bad.

The point is to see what the position produces.

The edge is available as a position of perception, but it is not comfortable.

To stand there is to lose the certainty that comes from belonging entirely to one narrative.

It is to understand that what feels like ground has a construction history.

In its place, there is something quieter.

Not a new story.

Not another total narrative to replace the one that has been seen through.

But the ability to recognize stories as stories and, in doing so, recover some degree of freedom from those that were never consciously chosen.

XVII. What Remains

From the edge, the question is no longer simply which narrative is true.

It becomes:

What is the structure through which truth is being formed?

Who benefits from that structure?

Whose position allows them to see it?

When does prominence become influence?

When does influence become power?

When does real power become a mythology of total control?

Who built the frame, and who is living inside the picture?

And once these questions are seen, another follows.

Perhaps the most difficult of all:

What is it to perceive, even briefly, before the structure takes hold?

Not as an escape from the world.

But as the ground from which it might be more honestly met.
